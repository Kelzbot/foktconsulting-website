If you've searched for "the new OMB rule" or "proposed changes to 2 CFR 200" lately, you've probably found a lot of commentary and not much clarity about what actually applies today. Here is where things stand as I write this, and what I'd do about it if I ran a nonprofit's finance team.

What was proposed

OMB published a proposed Uniform Grants Regulation on May 29, 2026, a rewrite of the rules that currently live in 2 CFR 200. The comment period closed on July 13, after close to half a million comments came in. OMB had wanted the new rule in force by October 1, 2026.

It isn't. The continuing resolution that funded the government included a provision saying the rule cannot be issued or finalized before December 11, 2026. So as of this writing, the proposal is still a proposal.

What applies right now

The 2024 revision of the Uniform Guidance is still the rulebook. That's the version that raised the Single Audit threshold to $1,000,000 in federal expenditures for fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024, and raised the de minimis indirect cost rate to 15 percent. If your awards are under 2 CFR 200 today, nothing about them changed on October 1 of this year.

That also means your current Single Audit, and the findings in it, will be tested against the 2024 rules. A proposed rule doesn't change what your auditor tests.

Why "when it lands" is the wrong question

The more useful thing to understand is how any new rule will arrive. Grant rules generally attach to awards. When a new version of the rules takes effect, it applies to new awards and to amendments that bring existing awards under it, not to every award you already hold all at once.

So for most organizations, the realistic picture for a while is two sets of rules running side by side: older awards under the 2024 Uniform Guidance and newer awards under whatever replaces it. Your finance team will need to know, for each award, which set it's under. That's a records question more than a regulatory one.

What I'd do now

Start with an award register if you don't already have one: every federal award and subaward, its start and end dates, the funder, the Assistance Listing number and the version of the rules written into its terms. Most organizations have this information spread across award letters and a grants calendar. Pulling it into one place takes a few hours and pays for itself the first time anyone asks which rules apply to what.

Read your award terms when you receive amendments, not just new awards. An amendment may be the moment an existing award moves under new requirements, and the change will be in the terms and conditions, not in a cover letter.

Don't rewrite your policies yet. It's tempting to start updating procurement thresholds or indirect cost procedures based on the proposal, but a proposal can change before it's final, sometimes a lot. Rewriting twice is worse than waiting. What you can do is note which of your written procedures quote specific thresholds or section numbers, so you know where the edits will go when a final rule exists.

And keep fixing your current findings. A corrective action plan written for the 2024 rules is still the plan your auditor will test next year, and a repeat finding is a repeat finding whatever version of the rules is coming.

Watching December 11

December 11 is the earliest date the rule could be issued or finalized under the current continuing resolution. That's a floor, not a release date. Congress could extend the restriction, OMB could take longer, or the final rule could differ from the proposal in ways that matter for your organization.

When a final rule does appear, the first things to look for are its effective date, whether it applies to new awards only or also to amendments, and which thresholds changed. I'll write that up when it happens. Until then, the 2024 Uniform Guidance governs your awards and your audit, and the most valuable preparation is knowing exactly which awards you hold and under what terms.
