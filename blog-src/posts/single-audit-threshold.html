Every year some nonprofits learn they needed a Single Audit after the deadline for filing one has passed. Usually it's an organization that grew, picked up a subaward or two through a state agency, and didn't realize the federal money from different sources added up. The rule is simple once you see how it counts.

The threshold

Under 2 CFR 200.501, a non-federal entity that expends $1,000,000 or more in federal awards during its fiscal year must have a Single Audit, or in some cases a program-specific audit. That figure applies to fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024. Before the 2024 revision of the Uniform Guidance, the threshold was $750,000.

Two words in that sentence do most of the work: expends, and federal.

Expended, not awarded

The threshold is about what you spend in the fiscal year, not the size of your awards. A three-year, $2.4 million award might only produce $700,000 of expenditures in your first year, which on its own wouldn't trigger an audit. A $900,000 award fully spent in one year, combined with a small grant from somewhere else, could.

The rules for when federal money counts as expended are in 2 CFR 200.502. Generally it's when the activity that the award pays for happens: incurring allowable costs, making subawards that the subrecipient spends, and so on. Some types of assistance, like loans and loan guarantees, have their own counting rules, and if you have them it's worth reading that section carefully.

Federal, wherever it came from

This is where organizations get caught. Federal money doesn't stop being federal when it passes through a state agency, a county or a larger nonprofit. If a state health department gives you a subaward funded by a federal grant, those expenditures count toward your $1,000,000 exactly as if the federal agency had funded you directly.

Your subaward agreements are supposed to tell you when the money is federal, including the Assistance Listing number. Check every grant agreement you have from a government or large intermediary. If it carries an Assistance Listing number, it's federal money, and it goes on your schedule of expenditures of federal awards.

Crossing it for the first time

The first-year problem is rarely the audit itself. It's that the organization's systems were built for a smaller, simpler funding base and haven't caught up with the rules that came with the money.

A few things tend to be missing. The schedule of expenditures of federal awards has to be prepared by the organization, accurately, by award and Assistance Listing number, and a lot of first-time auditees have never built one. Written procurement procedures are required under 2 CFR 200.318, and many smaller organizations don't have them. Time and effort documentation for staff charged to federal awards is often thin. I'll write about why first audits produce so many findings separately, but if you're near the threshold, those are the places to look first.

Plan the timing too. The Single Audit reporting package is due by the earlier of 30 days after you receive the auditor's report or nine months after your fiscal year ends. Audit firms with Single Audit experience book up, so finding one in month eight is harder than finding one in month two.

Below the threshold

If you spend less than $1,000,000 in federal awards in a year, you're exempt from the Single Audit requirement for that year. You're not exempt from the rules. Your records still have to be available for review by the federal agency, the pass-through entity and the Government Accountability Office, and a pass-through entity can still require monitoring, reviews or other assurances as a condition of its subaward.

Being below the threshold also doesn't mean your controls can wait. Organizations that grow past it usually do so quickly, often in a single year when one large award starts spending. The ones that handle their first audit well are usually the ones that built the procedures while they were still small enough for it to be easy.

A quick check

Pull every grant and contract agreement you received money under this fiscal year. Mark each one that carries an Assistance Listing number, or that says it's funded by a federal award. Add up what you expect to spend under those this year. If you're anywhere near $1,000,000, talk to an auditor now, while there's still time to fix things before they're tested.
