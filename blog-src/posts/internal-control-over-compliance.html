"Internal control over compliance" is the phrase that turns up in more Single Audit findings than any other, and it's one of the least explained. Ask ten finance staff what it means and you'll get ten versions of "having good policies." That's part of it, and it's the part that matters least.

Here's the plain-English version I use: who checks what, before the money moves, and can you prove it?

What the rule says

Under 2 CFR 200.303, a recipient of federal money has to establish, document and maintain effective internal control over the federal award that provides reasonable assurance it is managing the award in compliance with federal statutes, regulations and the award's terms. The rule points to two frameworks that internal control should be in line with: the Government Accountability Office's Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government, known as the Green Book, and the COSO Internal Control Integrated Framework.

It also asks you to evaluate and monitor your compliance, take prompt action when noncompliance is found, including on audit findings, and take reasonable steps to protect personally identifiable and other sensitive information.

You don't need to read the Green Book cover to cover to apply this. You need to understand what an auditor is checking when they test it.

Who checks what

A control is a step where someone, or something, checks that a requirement is met. A purchase is approved by someone with authority. A timesheet is signed by a supervisor who knows the work. A subrecipient invoice is reviewed against the budget before it's paid. Each of those is a control, and each is tied to a specific compliance requirement.

The important word is someone. A control that depends on "the finance team" rather than a named role tends not to happen, because everyone assumes someone else did it. And the person checking should usually be different from the person doing, so that one person's mistake isn't approved by the same person.

Before the money moves

Timing is where most control problems really sit. A review that happens after the payment, or after the charge posts to the award, can tell you something went wrong. It can't stop it. Auditors call these detective controls, and they're useful, but a process that relies only on them means problems are found after they've happened, which is the definition of a finding.

The strongest controls sit before the transaction completes: the purchase order can't be issued without the quote comparison attached, the charge can't post to the award without the allocation basis, the invoice can't be paid without the program manager's sign-off. If the requirement isn't met, the transaction waits.

Can you prove it

This is the part that turns a decent process into an audit finding. A control that happens but leaves no trace looks, to an auditor, exactly like a control that doesn't happen. If the approval was a verbal "go ahead" or an email reply nobody filed, there's nothing to sample.

When an auditor tests internal control over compliance, they typically select transactions and look for evidence that the control operated on each one: a signature, a system approval with a name and date, a checklist with references, a review note. The evidence has to be dated before the thing it controls. An approval signed after the payment went out shows the opposite of what you want it to.

Design and operation

Auditors look at controls in two ways. Design: if this control works as described, would it catch the problem it's meant to catch? Operation: did it actually work, consistently, through the year?

Policies speak to design. Evidence on transactions speaks to operation. A lot of organizations have well-designed controls on paper and no evidence they operated, which is why the policy manual so rarely saves anyone in a Single Audit.

A quick self-check

Take one compliance requirement that applies to your biggest program, say allowable costs. Write down, in one sentence, who checks a charge before it posts and what they check. Then pull ten charges from last month and look for the evidence of that check on each.

If you can find the sentence and the evidence, you have internal control over compliance for that requirement. If you can find the sentence but not the evidence, you have a policy. Auditors only count the first one.
