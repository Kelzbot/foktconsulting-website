A lot of nonprofit leaders treat the Single Audit like a private conversation between the organization and its auditor. The audit firm comes in, asks for documents, writes a report, and the report goes to the board and a few funders. That picture is out of date. Your findings, and your response to them, are public.

Where your audit goes

Every Single Audit reporting package is submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse, run by the General Services Administration at fac.gov. The package includes the financial statements, the schedule of expenditures of federal awards, the auditor's reports, the schedule of findings and questioned costs, your Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings and your corrective action plan.

The Clearinghouse makes this information publicly available. Anyone can search by organization name or identifier and read the findings and your corrective action plan, word for word, going back years. There is an exception that allows Indian tribes and tribal organizations to opt out of public availability of their reporting package, but for an ordinary nonprofit, the default is public.

Who actually looks

Federal agencies do. Before making an award, an agency is expected to assess the risk an applicant poses, and 2 CFR 200.206 lists reports and findings from Single Audits among the things it can consider. A program officer evaluating your application may pull up your last few years of findings in a few minutes, alongside your financial stability and your history with other awards.

Pass-through entities look too. If you receive federal money through a state agency or a larger nonprofit, they have to evaluate your risk before issuing a subaward and use it to decide how closely to monitor you. Your audit results are one of the main inputs. A clean audit can mean lighter monitoring. Repeat findings in procurement can mean the pass-through asks to see your purchase documentation every quarter.

Others can look as well, because public means public. Partner organizations deciding whether to bring you into a consortium can read it. So can a journalist, a board candidate doing due diligence, or a foundation that funds you alongside federal money.

What they're reading for

Most readers aren't auditors, and they don't read the whole package. They look at the summary of the auditor's results, which lists the type of opinion on each major program and whether material weaknesses or significant deficiencies were found. Then they skim the findings, looking at the labels, whether anything is a repeat, and whether there are questioned costs.

Then they read your response and your plan. This is the part people forget. The reader is trying to decide whether your organization understands the problem and has actually fixed it, and the only evidence they have is what you wrote.

Writing for a stranger

Once you know the corrective action plan is public, it changes how you write it. Whoever drafts your CAP is also writing the paragraph a future funder reads to decide whether your finance team has things handled.

A vague plan, "management will strengthen controls and provide training," tells that reader nothing, and in the absence of information people assume the worst. A specific plan that names who owns the fix, what changed in the process and from what date tells them you know your own operation.

The same goes for the management response. A response that blames a former employee will be readable long after that employee's replacement has fixed everything. A response that argues without evidence reads as defensive. A short, factual response that names the cause and the change reads as competent, which is the only impression worth leaving.

A useful exercise

Search for your own organization on fac.gov and read your last two or three audits as if you were a program officer who had never heard of you. Look at the labels, the repeats and your own responses. Then search for a peer organization and read theirs.

Most people who do this for the first time are surprised by two things: how easy it is to find, and how much the tone of the response changes their impression of the organization behind it.
