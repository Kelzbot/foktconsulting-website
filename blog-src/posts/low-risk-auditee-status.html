Low-risk auditee is a status most nonprofits only hear about when they lose it. It isn't an award or a certificate. It's a set of conditions in the Uniform Guidance that, if you meet them, lets your auditor test less of your federal spending. Lose it, and next year's audit gets bigger whether your programs changed or not.

What it changes

In a Single Audit, the auditor chooses major programs to test in depth, and those programs together have to cover a minimum share of your total federal expenditures. That's the percentage of coverage rule in 2 CFR 200.518. For most auditees, the major programs must cover at least 40 percent of federal expenditures. For a low-risk auditee, the floor drops to 20 percent.

Half the coverage often means fewer programs tested, which means fewer compliance requirements examined, fewer samples pulled and less fieldwork. Since audit fees mostly follow hours, it usually shows up in the bill.

There's a second effect that's harder to price. Fewer major programs means fewer places a finding can come from. An organization that's low risk tends to stay low risk, and one that isn't has more surface for the findings that keep it that way.

The conditions

The test is in 2 CFR 200.520, and an auditee has to meet all of it for each of the two preceding audit periods. In plain terms:

  • Single Audits were performed annually and submitted on time, in line with the requirements.
  • The auditor's opinions on the financial statements and on the schedule of expenditures of federal awards were unmodified, and there was no substantial doubt about the organization's ability to continue as a going concern.
  • The financial statement audit reported no deficiencies in internal control identified as material weaknesses.
  • None of the federal programs had audit findings of certain serious kinds in either of the preceding two years when it was audited as a Type A program, namely internal control deficiencies identified as material weaknesses, a modified opinion on a major program, or known or likely questioned costs above five percent of the program's expenditures for the year.

The rule's exact wording has more detail, including how it applies to Type A programs, and it's worth reading with your auditor. But the shape is clear: two clean, timely years.

What knocks you out

Notice what's not on the list. A significant deficiency doesn't disqualify you. Ordinary noncompliance that isn't material doesn't either, and neither do small questioned costs. The status survives a lot of routine findings.

What ends it is the severe end of the scale: a material weakness, a modified opinion on a major program, questioned costs over the five percent line, or a late or missing audit. And because the test looks back two years, one bad year costs you two audits of low-risk treatment, not one.

The repeat-finding trap

This is where repeat findings get expensive in a way people don't see coming. Say a material weakness in your largest program's reporting knocks you out of low-risk status this year. Next year, without the status, the auditor must cover more of your spending, often testing programs that weren't tested before. If those programs have their own gaps, new findings appear, and the clean two-year run you need to get back in moves further away.

Organizations caught in that loop tend to see their audits grow year on year, with findings in areas nobody had looked at before. It isn't that things got worse. It's that more of what was already there got tested.

Keeping or regaining it

The fastest way back is boring and specific. Submit on time every year. Treat any finding that's labelled a material weakness, or comes with questioned costs near the five percent line, as the priority, because those are the ones that cost you the status. Fix them at the step where the transaction happens, so the follow-up sample is clean, and test your own fix a few months in so you're not relying on hope.

Ask your auditor where you stand. Whether you qualify is a determination they make at the start of each audit, and they can tell you exactly which of your recent findings, if any, is holding you out.
