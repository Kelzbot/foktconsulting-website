At the end of an audit, some organizations receive two documents with problems in them. One is the audit report, with its schedule of findings. The other is a letter, often called a management letter, with a list of observations and recommendations. They look similar. They are treated very differently, and the difference is worth understanding before the letter's items become next year's findings.

Reported findings

In a Single Audit, certain matters must be reported as audit findings. 2 CFR 200.516 sets out what the auditor has to report, including significant deficiencies and material weaknesses in internal control over major programs, material noncompliance with the requirements of those programs and questioned costs above the rule's threshold.

Reported findings go in the schedule of findings and questioned costs, which is part of the reporting package submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. They're public, they need a corrective action plan, they get management decisions from your funders, and they're followed up in the next audit. Everything in the earlier posts in this series is about them.

Management letter comments

Auditors notice more than they're required to report. A deficiency that isn't serious enough to be a significant deficiency, an inefficiency in a process, a control that worked but was fragile, a recommendation about something outside the scope of the compliance testing. Auditing standards let the auditor communicate these to management in other ways, and the management letter is the usual vehicle.

The management letter is not part of the reporting package. It isn't submitted to the Clearinghouse, it isn't public, and it doesn't come with a formal management decision. It's a private communication between the auditor and the organization.

How the auditor decides which is which

It comes down to severity and scope. A deficiency in internal control over a major program is graded: is there a reasonable possibility it could let material noncompliance through (material weakness), or is it important enough to deserve governance attention even if less severe (significant deficiency)? If it meets one of those bars, it's reported. If it's a deficiency that doesn't, it can go in the letter.

Scope matters too. Issues in programs that weren't tested as major programs, or in areas outside the compliance requirements in scope, generally aren't reported as Single Audit findings, even if the auditor noticed them. They may well show up in the letter.

Why the letter deserves attention

Because management letter comments are the findings of the future. A minor control weakness this year can grow into a significant deficiency next year, especially if transaction volume rises or a key person leaves. An issue on a program that wasn't major this year can become a reported finding the year that program is selected.

Auditors also remember their letters. If they flagged something informally and it's still there twelve months later, it's harder to argue the next time that it's minor, because the organization was told and didn't act. That history can push the classification up a grade.

And the fixes are almost always cheaper at this stage. A management letter comment usually describes a small gap with no questioned costs and nothing on the public record. Closing it costs a procedure update and a little discipline, and no funder ever reads about it.

What to do with your letter

Treat each comment like a small finding. Give it an owner, decide what you'll change and by when, and check a few months later that it happened. Keep a short note of what you did. You don't need a formal corrective action plan, but when the auditor arrives next year and asks about last year's letter, a one-page summary of what changed will help the conversation a lot.

The best outcome for a management letter comment is that it never appears anywhere else, and the only way that happens is if it's fixed while it's still private.
