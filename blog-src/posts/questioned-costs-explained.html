The finding has a number in it, say $64,210 in questioned costs, and the first reaction is usually to start working out where the money for a refund will come from. Slow down. A questioned cost is not a bill. It is the auditor saying they couldn't confirm that money was spent the way the rules allow. What happens to it is decided later, by someone else, and documentation is what usually decides it.

What a questioned cost is

The Uniform Guidance defines a questioned cost in 2 CFR 200.84. In plain terms, it's a cost the auditor questions because of an audit finding, for one of three reasons. The cost may break a statute, regulation or the terms of the award. It may lack adequate documentation at the time of the audit. Or it may look unreasonable, in that it doesn't reflect what a prudent person would have spent in the circumstances.

The middle one is by far the most common in nonprofit audits. The cost might be perfectly allowable. The auditor just couldn't see the evidence that it was.

Known and likely

Auditors report known questioned costs, the actual dollars they found in the items they tested. They also have to consider likely questioned costs, their best estimate of the total across the whole population the sample came from. If a sample of $200,000 in payroll charges turns up $20,000 without support, the likely questioned costs across $2 million of payroll could be much larger.

Known questioned costs above a threshold set in the rule are reported in the finding. Likely questioned costs matter in a different way: if they exceed five percent of a major program's expenditures, that is one of the things that keeps an organization from qualifying as a low-risk auditee.

Questioned is not disallowed

This is the distinction that matters. A disallowed cost is a charge that the federal agency or pass-through entity has determined to be unallowable. The auditor can question. Only the funder can disallow.

That determination comes in the management decision, which the agency or pass-through entity issues after reviewing the finding and your corrective action plan. It says whether the questioned costs are allowed or disallowed and, if they're disallowed, what you are expected to do about them, which can include repayment, an offset against future payments or an adjustment to the award.

Between the audit report and the management decision, you have an opportunity that a lot of organizations don't use.

Documentation is usually what saves the money

If the costs were questioned because support was missing, and the support exists, find it. Timesheets that were filed in the wrong place, approvals that lived in an email thread, allocation calculations sitting in someone's spreadsheet: any of these can resolve a questioned cost if you can show them to the funder.

Be thorough and organized about it. List each questioned item, attach the documentation that supports it, and explain how it shows the cost was allowable, allocable to the award and reasonable. Send it to the funder with your corrective action plan or as soon as you have it, so it's in front of them when they write the management decision.

Be honest about what you can't support. If some items genuinely lack documentation, say so. The credibility you build by conceding the items you can't support makes the items you can support more persuasive.

When the cost really was unallowable

Sometimes the documentation shows the auditor was right. An alcohol line on a conference invoice, a cost charged to the wrong award, an expense outside the period of performance. In those cases the cost is likely to be disallowed, and the practical conversation with the funder is about how it gets resolved. Funders generally have options short of a cheque, and some will discuss how and when the amount is repaid or adjusted. Ask, and ask early.

What you shouldn't do is move the cost to another federal award to make it go away. A cost that's unallowable on one award is unallowable on the others too, and shifting it creates a fresh problem.

Preventing the next one

Most questioned costs in nonprofits come from a small set of places: payroll charged without support for how time was split across awards, allocations without a documented basis, and purchases without the paperwork the procurement rules require. Each of those is a process gap, not an accounting error.

The fix is to make the documentation part of the transaction, not something gathered afterwards. If the support has to be attached before a charge can be posted to the award, the question never comes up, because the evidence the auditor wants is sitting on the transaction they sample.
