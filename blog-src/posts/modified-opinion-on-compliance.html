Most of a Single Audit report is detail. One line of it is a headline. For each major program, the auditor gives an opinion on whether you complied, in all material respects, with the compliance requirements that could have a direct and material effect on that program. When that opinion is anything other than clean, it's the first thing a reader notices.

The four kinds of opinion

Unmodified is the clean one. The auditor concluded you complied, in all material respects, with the requirements tested. You can have findings and still get an unmodified opinion, because many findings are about internal control or are not material to compliance as a whole.

Qualified means you complied in all material respects except for something specific. The auditor found material noncompliance with one or more requirements, but it isn't pervasive. A qualified opinion usually names the requirement, for example "except for the noncompliance described in finding 2025-002 regarding subrecipient monitoring."

Adverse means the noncompliance is both material and pervasive. The auditor is saying the program, overall, wasn't run in compliance with its requirements. This is rare, and serious.

Disclaimer means the auditor couldn't form an opinion, usually because they couldn't get enough evidence. Records were missing, systems couldn't produce what was needed, or the scope was limited in some way. A disclaimer isn't a statement that you didn't comply. It's a statement that nobody can tell, which funders tend to read just as badly.

Where readers find it

The auditor's opinion on each major program appears in the report on compliance, and it's summarized in the summary of the auditor's results at the start of the schedule of findings and questioned costs. That summary, required under 2 CFR 200.515, lists the type of report the auditor issued on compliance for each major program, along with whether material weaknesses or significant deficiencies were found.

That's exactly where a program officer or a pass-through entity looks first when they pull your audit from the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. A short list of programs with "unmodified" next to each tells them a lot in a few seconds. "Qualified" makes them read on.

Why it matters

A modified opinion on a major program is one of the conditions that keeps an organization from qualifying as a low-risk auditee, and the low-risk test looks back two years. So one qualified opinion widens your audit scope for the next two audits.

It's also one of the signals federal agencies look for when they assess risk before an award. The agency is weighing whether you can manage federal money in line with the rules, and a modified opinion is the auditor saying, formally, that in at least one area you didn't. That can lead to specific conditions on new or existing awards under 2 CFR 200.208, like additional reporting, more frequent monitoring or a move to reimbursement payments.

And it's on the public record, next to your response.

How it happens

Most qualified opinions trace back to one compliance requirement where the problem was widespread in the sample. Allowable costs where a large share of payroll lacked support. Subrecipient monitoring where none of the subawards had a documented risk assessment. Reporting where the federal financial reports couldn't be reconciled to the books.

Disclaimers tend to come from records problems: an organization that changed accounting systems mid-year and lost history, or a program run out of a field office where documents never made it back.

What to do next

Read the finding the opinion points to. It will tell you which requirement failed, how widely and why. That's the single most important finding in your report, and it's where your corrective action effort should go first.

Talk to the funder before they come to you. A short, factual note explaining what happened, what you've changed and when the change took effect does more for the relationship than waiting for the management decision.

And plan for the follow-up. That program will almost certainly be a major program again next year. The auditor will test the same requirement on a fresh sample, and the opinion on it will depend on whether the change you made shows up on the transactions they pick.
