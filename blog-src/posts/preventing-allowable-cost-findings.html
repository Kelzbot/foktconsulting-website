Allowable costs is the compliance requirement that produces more Single Audit findings than any other, and it is the most preventable. Almost every allowable-cost finding I have seen describes a charge that was probably fine. It just couldn't be proven fine when the auditor asked.

The trick is to ask the auditor's questions before the charge posts, not after.

The questions the auditor asks

The general rule for whether a cost can be charged to a federal award is in 2 CFR 200.403. To be allowable, a cost has to meet several factors at once. It has to be necessary and reasonable for the award, and allocable to it. It has to follow any limits in the cost principles or the award terms, and be treated consistently with how you treat similar costs on your other funding. It can't be counted as cost sharing on another award, and it has to be adequately documented.

When an auditor tests a sample of charges, they're working through those factors for each item. In practice three of them generate nearly all the findings.

Allocable

A cost is allocable to an award if the award gets the benefit of it in proportion to what it's charged, as 2 CFR 200.405 describes. This is where shared costs get into trouble. Rent, utilities, a program manager who works across three grants. The charge to each award needs a basis, written down before or when the charge is made, that explains why that award pays that share.

Reasonable

Reasonable means a prudent person would have paid it in the circumstances, under 2 CFR 200.404. It rarely produces findings on its own, but it shows up when something looks unusual and there's no explanation: a premium hotel rate, a vendor that was much more expensive than the alternatives, a consultant fee with no comparison.

Adequately documented

This is the big one, and it's the factor that turns probably-fine charges into questioned costs. If the support isn't there when the auditor asks, the charge fails, even if it was entirely legitimate.

Where it breaks: payroll

Salaries are usually the largest cost on a federal award and the most common source of allowable-cost findings. The standard in 2 CFR 200.430(i) is that charges for salaries and wages have to be based on records that accurately reflect the work performed, supported by a system of internal control that gives reasonable assurance the charges are accurate, allowable and properly allocated.

The rule doesn't dictate a timesheet format. What it does require is that the split of someone's pay across awards reflects how they actually spent their time, not a budget percentage set at the start of the year and never revisited. The classic finding says employees were charged according to the budget, and nothing showed the budget matched the work.

The practical fix is a periodic record, signed by the employee or a supervisor with first-hand knowledge, that shows how time was really split, and an adjustment when it differs from what was charged. Then keep that record where the auditor can find it next to the payroll charge.

Where it breaks: allocations

Shared costs spread across awards are the second big source. The finding usually says the allocation basis wasn't documented, or the allocation in the ledger can't be reproduced from the basis. A spreadsheet somewhere did the math once, and nobody saved the version that was used.

Write down the method, keep the calculation for each period with the journal entry it produced, and have someone other than the preparer approve it. If the method changes, record when and why.

Testing before posting

Prevention means moving the auditor's questions to the point where a charge enters the award. Before a cost posts, someone confirms it has an invoice or equivalent, an approval from the right person, a basis for the amount charged to this award, and that it falls inside the award's period of performance and budget categories. If any of those is missing, the charge waits.

That sounds slow. In practice it's faster than the alternative, which is spending the weeks before fieldwork hunting for support for charges made eleven months ago, and losing the ones you can't find.

The simplest version is a checklist attached to the payment request or journal entry, where each line has to be ticked with a reference before approval. Better still is a system where the charge can't be posted without the attachments. Either way, the auditor who samples that charge next year finds the evidence sitting on the transaction, because it was never allowed to post without it.
