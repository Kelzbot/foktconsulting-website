A first-year finding tells a funder that something was missing. A repeat finding tells them something more uncomfortable: the organization knew about the problem, wrote a plan to fix it, and the plan didn't work. That is why the word repeat carries more weight in a Single Audit report than almost any other.

How a finding becomes a repeat

Every Single Audit finding has to say whether it was reported in the prior year, and if so, give the prior finding's reference number. The rule asks for it in 2 CFR 200.516 so that readers can see patterns. Finding 2025-003 with "repeat of 2024-002" next to it is a different document from 2025-003 standing alone.

The auditor gets there through follow-up. At each audit, they review your Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings, where you report the status of every prior finding, and they test whether the corrective action actually happened. If they pull a fresh sample from the area and find the same gap, it's a repeat.

Why the second one matters more

A funder reading a first-time finding can reasonably think: they grew, a process didn't keep up, they've written a plan. Organizations get findings, especially in their first Single Audit, and a reasonable program officer knows that.

A repeat removes that charitable reading. It suggests the problem is not the transaction or even the process, but the organization's ability to change a process when told to. That is a management signal, and management capacity is exactly what federal agencies are assessing when they review an applicant's risk before an award.

Repeats also have mechanical consequences. Certain findings in either of the two preceding years keep you from qualifying as a low-risk auditee, so a repeat keeps you out of that status for longer, which keeps your audit scope wider and your fieldwork longer. And under 2 CFR 200.208, a federal agency or pass-through entity can impose specific conditions on an award based on an organization's history of compliance. Conditions like moving you from advance payments to reimbursement, requiring additional reports or requiring prior approval for things you used to decide yourself all become easier to justify when the same finding keeps coming back.

The usual reason findings repeat

In my experience, most repeat findings aren't caused by a bad policy. The policy usually gets updated after the first finding. The problem is that nothing changes at the step where the transaction happens, and nothing records that the step happened.

Here's what that looks like. The finding says purchases lacked documented price comparisons. The organization updates its procurement policy to require them. The policy is approved by the board and circulated. Then the purchasing process carries on exactly as before, because the requisition form doesn't ask for the comparison, the accounting system doesn't require it before a purchase order is issued, and nobody checks. Next year's auditor samples forty purchases, finds the same gap in a dozen of them, and the finding comes back.

The policy got fixed. The step didn't. The auditor never tests policies by reading them. They test whether the step happened, on a sample, with evidence.

Other causes worth checking

Timing is the next most common one. If the fix went live in month ten of the fiscal year, the auditor's sample is drawn from the whole year, and most of it predates the change. Your plan might be working perfectly and the finding still repeats, because most of the sampled transactions came from before it existed.

Another is a fix that covers the transactions the auditor happened to sample but not the ones they didn't. If the finding was about payroll charges on one award and the fix only changed that award, the next sample may come from another.

And sometimes the owner leaves. A corrective action that lives in one person's habits, rather than in the system or the form, leaves with them.

Breaking the cycle

Start by reading the original finding's cause and asking whether your fix changed a step or just a document. If it changed a document, find the step. Put the requirement into the form, the system or the approval route, so the transaction can't move without it and leaves a record when it does.

Then test it yourself before the auditor does. After the change has run for a couple of months, pull a sample of transactions from after the change date and check each one carries the evidence. If you find gaps, you've found them early enough to fix.

Finally, be honest on the Summary Schedule. If the fix only went live late in the year, say so and give the date. "Partially corrected, new approval step in place since October 1, testing ongoing" reads far better to a funder than "fully corrected" followed by a repeat finding that contradicts it.
