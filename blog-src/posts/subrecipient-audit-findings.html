When your organization passes federal money to another organization, you stop being only a recipient. You become a pass-through entity, with a set of duties that a lot of nonprofits take on without noticing. One of them is what happens when your subrecipient's own audit has a finding.

The short answer to the question in the title is yes. Their finding becomes your follow-up, and if you don't follow up, it becomes your finding.

What a pass-through entity owes

The requirements are in 2 CFR 200.332. Before issuing a subaward, you have to evaluate each subrecipient's risk of noncompliance, considering things like their prior experience with similar awards, the results of previous audits, whether they have new personnel or new systems, and the extent of federal monitoring they're already under. The subaward agreement has to include specific information about the federal award. Then you have to monitor their activities to make sure the subaward is used for authorized purposes and in line with the terms.

Audits are part of that monitoring. You have to verify that each subrecipient that's required to have a Single Audit gets one. And you have to follow up and make sure the subrecipient takes timely and appropriate action on deficiencies found through audits, on-site reviews and your other monitoring that pertain to the subaward you gave them.

The management decision is yours to issue

When a subrecipient's Single Audit has a finding that relates to the money you passed to them, the federal agency doesn't resolve it. You do. Under 2 CFR 200.521, the pass-through entity is responsible for issuing a management decision on audit findings that relate to its subaward, generally within six months of the audit being accepted by the Federal Audit Clearinghouse.

That decision should say whether the finding is sustained, what the subrecipient is expected to do, including repaying disallowed costs if any, and a timetable for follow-up. It's a real document with real consequences, and a lot of pass-through entities, especially smaller nonprofits passing money to even smaller ones, have never written one.

You also have to consider whether the subrecipient's findings mean you need to adjust your own records. If your subrecipient charged unallowable costs to the subaward, those costs flowed through your award too.

How it becomes your finding

When your auditor tests subrecipient monitoring, the M requirement in the Compliance Supplement, they typically select subawards and look for evidence that you did each step: the risk assessment before the subaward was signed, the required information in the agreement, monitoring during the award, verification that the subrecipient was audited if required, and follow-up on any findings.

If a subrecipient's Single Audit had a finding on your subaward and there's no management decision from you and no record of follow-up, that's a gap your auditor can report. So your subrecipient's problem has become two problems: theirs, which they still have to fix, and yours, which is that you didn't do anything about theirs.

Finding out in the first place

The awkward part is that you can't follow up on findings you don't know about. A lot of pass-through entities only check whether a subrecipient had an audit, not what it found.

The Federal Audit Clearinghouse makes this easier than it used to be. You can search for each subrecipient, read their reporting package, and check for findings that mention your program or your subaward. Do it every year, after their audit is due, and record that you did, even when the answer is "no findings relating to our subaward." That record is the evidence your auditor will ask for.

A workable routine

Keep a simple register of every subrecipient: their fiscal year-end, whether they're expected to need a Single Audit, when it's due and when you checked it. When a finding relates to your subaward, ask the subrecipient for their corrective action plan, issue a short management decision that sustains or clears the finding and says what you expect by when, and put a reminder to check whether it was done.

And feed what you learn back into your risk assessment. A subrecipient with repeat findings in procurement is one whose purchases you should be looking at more closely next year, and your file should show that you noticed.
