Two findings can describe almost the same problem and carry different labels. One says significant deficiency, the other says material weakness, and the second one does a lot more damage than its wording suggests. If you have ever wondered why your auditor spent twenty minutes arguing about one word on a call, this is the word.

The three kinds of findings

People often ask what the three types of audit findings are. In a Single Audit the useful answer is that findings fall into two families, plus money.

The first family is about internal control over compliance: whether your processes would catch a problem. These are graded as either a significant deficiency or a material weakness. The second family is about compliance itself: whether the rules were actually followed, which shows up as noncompliance, sometimes material noncompliance. And then there are questioned costs, the dollars the auditor could not support.

One finding can sit in more than one family. A finding about unsupported payroll charges can be a material weakness in internal control, material noncompliance with the allowable costs requirement and a questioned cost, all in the same paragraph. When you read the header of a finding, look for each of those labels separately, because they have different consequences.

How control problems get graded

Start with the plain idea of a deficiency. A deficiency in internal control over compliance exists when the way a process is designed, or the way it is actually run, does not let your staff prevent a compliance problem or catch and fix it in time. Most organizations have plenty of these, and most of them never reach a report.

A significant deficiency is a deficiency that is less severe than a material weakness but important enough that the people charged with governance, usually your board or audit committee, should hear about it.

A material weakness is the serious grade. It means there is a reasonable possibility that material noncompliance with a compliance requirement will not be prevented, or will not be caught and corrected in time. Notice what that definition does not require. Nothing has to have gone wrong yet. A material weakness is a judgement about what your controls would let through, and the auditor can reach it even when the sample happened to be clean.

What pushes a finding up a grade

Auditors weigh how likely it is that the control fails and how big the problem could be if it does. In practice a few things tend to tip the label.

The first is the absence of any control at all, as opposed to a control that exists and was occasionally skipped. If there is no review of subrecipient reports, ever, the auditor has nothing to say "usually works" about. The second is how much money runs through the process. A gap in the approval of small purchases on a minor program reads very differently from a gap in the approval of every payroll charge on your largest award.

Repetition matters too. A control problem that was reported last year, that you said you fixed, and that shows up again in the sample is hard to describe as anything less than serious, because the evidence now says your correction process does not work either.

Why the label matters more than it looks

The most direct cost is low-risk auditee status. Under 2 CFR 200.520, an organization can only qualify as a low-risk auditee if, among other conditions, none of its federal programs had an internal control deficiency classified as a material weakness in either of the two preceding audit years. A significant deficiency doesn't knock you out. A material weakness does, for two years.

Losing low-risk status means the auditor must test a bigger share of your federal spending as major programs, which means more fieldwork, which is usually reflected in the fee. It also changes how funders read you. Federal agencies are expected to review audit results when they assess the risk of a new award, and pass-through entities do the same when deciding how closely to monitor you. "Material weakness" is one of the phrases those reviewers are trained to look for.

Can you argue the label?

Before the report is final, yes, within reason. The auditor's classification is a professional judgement, and judgement responds to facts. If a compensating control exists that the auditor didn't see, for example a monthly review by the finance director that would catch the same error the missing step should have caught, show it with evidence that it actually ran during the year. Explaining what you intended to do will not move the grade. Showing what you did can.

What doesn't help is arguing about the word itself. Auditors hear "this seems harsh" often, and it carries no weight. The productive conversation is about the facts behind the two tests, how likely the failure is and how large it could be.

Responding to each grade

For a significant deficiency, the corrective action plan can usually be focused: fix the step, document it and show it running. For a material weakness, the plan needs to show that the whole process has a control now, not just the transaction type that was sampled, because the auditor's concern was what the process would let through, not the specific items they found.

Either way the follow-up test next year is the same. The auditor will pull a fresh sample from after your fix and look for the evidence your plan promised. If the plan said the controller reviews every subrecipient report before payment, they will look for the controller's review on every report in the sample, with a date that comes before the payment.
