The audit report arrives, and somewhere past the financial statements there is a section called the Schedule of Findings and Questioned Costs. Under it sits a numbered item, something like 2025-001, and a paragraph full of words like criteria and condition. If you run a nonprofit, the first question is usually whether this means someone thinks money was stolen.

Almost always it doesn't. A Single Audit finding is the auditor writing down a gap between what the federal rules required and what actually happened, in a way that can be checked. Most findings are about an approval that left no record, or a process that nobody ever wrote down. They matter, and they can follow you for years, but they are a statement about your controls, not an accusation.

Where findings come from

A Single Audit is the audit required by Subpart F of the Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR 200. For fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024, a nonprofit that spends $1,000,000 or more in federal awards in a year needs one. Before that revision the threshold was $750,000, which is why a lot of organizations that crossed the old line and not the new one are still catching up on what applies to them.

The Single Audit has two halves. One is the normal audit of your financial statements. The other looks at your biggest or riskiest federal programs, the auditor's "major programs", and asks two questions about each. Did you comply with the requirements that could have a direct and material effect on the program, and do you have internal controls that would catch it if you didn't?

Findings come from that second half. 2 CFR 200.516 tells the auditor what has to be reported, and in plain terms it covers serious weaknesses in your internal control over a major program, material noncompliance with the program's requirements, and questioned costs above a dollar threshold set in the rule. A finding can involve no money at all. If every dollar was spent correctly but nobody approved the spending the way your own policy says they must, that can still be a finding, because the control failed even though the outcome happened to be fine.

How to read one, part by part

Every finding is built from the same parts, because the rule lists them. Once you know what each part is for, the paragraph that looked like legal language turns into something you can work with.

PartWhat it tells you
Federal programWhich award and Assistance Listing number the finding sits on, and the agency or pass-through entity that funds it.
CriteriaThe rule, award term or your own policy that set the standard. Usually a citation to 2 CFR 200 or the award.
ConditionWhat the auditor actually found when they tested you.
CauseWhy it happened. This is the part your fix has to answer.
EffectWhat could go wrong, or did, because of the gap.
Questioned costsAny dollars the auditor could not support, or "none".
ContextHow big the sample was and how many items failed, so a reader can judge whether it is isolated or systemic.
Repeat findingWhether the same issue was reported last year, with the prior reference number.
RecommendationWhat the auditor suggests.
Views of responsible officialsYour response, in your words.

Read the condition and the context together before anything else. "Three of forty sampled transactions lacked documented approval" and "thirty-one of forty sampled transactions lacked documented approval" are the same finding on paper and a completely different conversation with your funder.

Then read the cause carefully, because it is the line most organizations skim and the one that decides whether the finding comes back. If the cause says "oversight" or "staff turnover", that is a description of the moment it went wrong, not of the reason. The real cause is usually that there is no step in the process that forces the approval to happen and leaves a record that it did.

What a finding is not

It is not a fine. Nobody sends an invoice because a finding exists. Money only comes back into question when there are questioned costs, and even then the federal agency or pass-through entity decides what happens to them later, in its management decision.

It is not the auditor's opinion of your mission or your staff. Auditors test what the rules ask them to test, on a sample, and write up what they see. A strong program team and a finding on procurement can sit side by side in the same report, and often do.

And it is not private. The whole reporting package, findings included, goes to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse, where anyone can search it by organization name. I'll come back to that in its own post, because it changes how you should write your response.

What happens after the report lands

You write a corrective action plan for every finding. That's your job, not the auditor's: the plan names the person responsible, what will change and when it will be done, and it goes in with the reporting package. The federal agency or pass-through entity then reviews the finding and your plan and issues a management decision, which tells you whether they accept the plan and whether any money is at stake.

The real test comes a year later. At the next audit, the auditor follows up on every prior finding and checks your Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings, the document where you say what you did about each one. If the fix was a policy update and nothing changed in the actual process, next year's sample finds the same gap and the finding comes back with "repeat" next to it. That word is what funders notice.

So the useful way to read a finding is as a very specific work order, where the condition tells you how far off you are and the cause tells you which step in your process is missing. The paragraph that arrived looking like an accusation is mostly a description of a missing signature line.
