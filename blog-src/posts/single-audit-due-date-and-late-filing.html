The Single Audit deadline is one of the few dates in federal grants that people get wrong in both directions. Some organizations think they have until nine months after year-end no matter what. Others think the clock only starts when the auditor finishes. The rule is a little stricter than either.

The deadline

Under 2 CFR 200.512(a), the audit has to be completed and the data collection form and reporting package submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse by the earlier of two dates: 30 calendar days after you receive the auditor's report, or nine months after the end of the audit period.

The word that matters is earlier. If your fiscal year ends June 30, the outside date is March 31. But if your auditor delivers the report on December 15, you have until January 14, not March 31. Organizations that get their audit done early and then let the submission sit are often late without realizing it.

The rule allows a cognizant or oversight agency to agree to a longer period in specific circumstances, and agencies have issued broader extensions in unusual years. Don't plan on one. If you think you'll need it, ask early and get the answer in writing.

Who has to do what

The submission is the organization's responsibility, not the auditor's. The auditor certifies their part of the data collection form, but the auditee submits it and certifies the rest. In practice the auditor often helps with the mechanics, which is how some organizations come to believe it's the auditor's job. If the auditor is late with their part, you're still the one who's late.

Build the timeline backwards. Agree with your auditor on when fieldwork starts, when the draft report arrives and how long you need to review it, prepare the corrective action plan and the Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings, and get the board's sign-off if your governance requires it. The 30-day clock starts when you receive the final report, so the documents you prepare should be nearly done by then.

What late filing costs

The immediate consequence is your low-risk auditee status. Under 2 CFR 200.520, to qualify as low risk you need to have had Single Audits performed on an annual basis, in accordance with the requirements, in each of the two preceding audit periods, and that includes submitting on time. A late submission takes you out of low-risk status, which means a wider audit next time and usually a larger fee.

Federal agencies can also act. 2 CFR 200.505 describes sanctions when an organization hasn't had an audit done properly, and they include withholding a percentage of federal awards until the audit is completed, withholding or disallowing the costs of audits that weren't done properly, suspending federal awards until the audit is done, and other actions under the remedies for noncompliance. Agencies don't reach for these every time a filing is late, but the authority is there, and repeated or long delays make it more likely.

Pass-through entities feel the delay as well. They need your audit to complete their own subrecipient monitoring, so a late audit can mean more questions, more document requests or delayed payments while they wait.

It stays on the record

Your submission date is part of what's visible on the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. A program officer reviewing your risk before an award can see not just your findings but whether your audits arrive when they should. One late year with a reason is understandable. A pattern of them reads as an organization that struggles to get basic compliance tasks done on time, and that reading spills over onto everything else.

If you're already late

Submit as soon as you can. A late audit is better than a missing one, and the consequences get worse the longer it's outstanding. Tell your federal agencies and pass-through entities before they find out, explain why, and give them a date you're confident you'll meet.

Then fix whatever made it late. If it was the audit firm's schedule, book next year's fieldwork now. If it was your own books, the problem is usually the year-end close and the schedule of expenditures of federal awards, and those are worth fixing before the next year-end, not during it. Next year's submission date will be read alongside this year's, and on time is the only thing that changes the story.
