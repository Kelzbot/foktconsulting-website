Ask an auditor what they test in a Single Audit and you'll eventually hear about "the compliance requirements," usually referred to by letter. "We're testing B, H and L on that program." It sounds like code. It's actually a short, stable list, and knowing it lets you follow the conversation and prepare for it.

The list

The OMB Compliance Supplement groups compliance requirements into types, labelled A to N in Part 3. Two letters, D and K, are reserved and not used, which is why the count is twelve rather than fourteen.

LetterRequirementIn plain English
AActivities allowed or unallowedWas the money spent on what the program pays for?
BAllowable costs/cost principlesDoes each charge meet the cost principles: allocable, reasonable, documented?
CCash managementDid you draw federal cash only when you needed it, and handle advances correctly?
EEligibilityDid the people or organizations served actually qualify?
FEquipment and real property managementIs property bought with federal money tracked, inventoried and disposed of properly?
GMatching, level of effort, earmarkingDid you contribute the required match, keep up required spending and spend required shares on the right things?
HPeriod of performanceWere costs incurred within the dates the award covers?
IProcurement and suspension and debarmentDid purchases follow the procurement standards, and were vendors checked against exclusion lists?
JProgram incomeWas income earned by the program recorded and used according to the award?
LReportingWere required financial and performance reports accurate, complete and on time?
MSubrecipient monitoringDid you assess, monitor and follow up on the organizations you passed money to?
NSpecial tests and provisionsProgram-specific rules that don't fit anywhere else.

Your auditor won't test all twelve

This is the part that matters most for preparation. For any given program, only some of these requirements are subject to audit, and the Compliance Supplement's Part 2 matrix tells you which. A "Y" next to your program's Assistance Listing number under a letter means that requirement is in scope. A blank means the auditor generally won't test it for that program.

OMB keeps the list for each program short on purpose. Since the 2019 edition, it has generally limited the number of requirements identified for a program to the ones most likely to matter, at most six for most programs. So instead of preparing for twelve areas across every grant, you're usually preparing for a few specific ones on a few specific programs.

For programs that aren't listed in the Supplement, the auditor uses Part 7 and the award terms to work out which requirements could have a direct and material effect. That usually means a conversation at planning, and it's worth having early.

The ones that produce the most findings

In nonprofit audits, B (allowable costs) is the most common source of findings by a distance, mostly through payroll and allocation documentation. M (subrecipient monitoring) is close behind for any organization that passes money on. I (procurement) catches organizations that buy things through federal awards without the documentation the procurement standards require, and L (reporting) catches reports that can't be traced back to the books.

N deserves special attention, because special tests are unique to each program and can't be learned from general grants training. If your program has a Part 4 section with special tests, read it line by line. It's the requirement most likely to surprise a team that's otherwise well prepared.

Using the list

Make a small table of your own: your likely major programs down the side, the letters across the top, and a mark wherever the matrix says "Y". Then, for each mark, pull a handful of your own transactions or records and test them the way the Supplement's suggested procedures describe.

That table is the scope of your audit, drawn before your auditor draws it. Anything that fails your own test is a finding you can fix before it's written.
