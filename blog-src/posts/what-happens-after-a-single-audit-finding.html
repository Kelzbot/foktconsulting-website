The audit is over, the report is signed, and there is a finding in it. The question people ask next is usually "what happens now?" and the honest answer is that very little happens quickly, and then one thing happens very precisely a year later. Knowing the sequence helps you spend the quiet months well.

Step one: the reporting package goes in

Your Single Audit reporting package goes to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse along with the data collection form. Under 2 CFR 200.512, it is due by the earlier of 30 calendar days after you receive the auditor's report or nine months after the end of your fiscal year.

The package includes the financial statements, the schedule of expenditures of federal awards, the auditor's reports, the Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings and your corrective action plan. So by the time anyone at a federal agency reads your finding, your plan for fixing it is sitting right next to it. That's why the plan deserves as much care as the response you give the auditor.

Step two: the funder reviews it

The federal agency that funds the program, or the pass-through entity if your money comes through a state or another nonprofit, reviews the findings that relate to its awards. For direct awards, the federal agency is responsible. For subawards, the pass-through entity issues the decision on findings that relate to the money it gave you.

Step three: the management decision

The management decision is the funder's formal response to your finding. Under 2 CFR 200.521, it should be issued within six months of the audit being accepted by the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. It should say whether the finding is sustained, why, and what you are expected to do, which can include repaying disallowed costs, making financial adjustments or taking other action, along with a timetable for follow-up.

When it arrives, read three things. First, whether the finding is sustained at all. Second, what happens to any questioned costs, because this is the document that turns "questioned" into "disallowed" or clears them. Third, whether the funder accepted your corrective action plan or wants something different, and by when.

In practice the timing varies a lot. Some agencies are prompt. Some decisions arrive late, and some findings never get a decision at all. That doesn't mean the finding went away. It means you keep reporting on it.

Step four: you fix it

The rule expects corrective action to start as soon as you receive the audit report, not when the management decision arrives. That's the part organizations most often get wrong. They wait for the funder to respond, the response takes five months, and by then half of next year's audit period has passed with the old process still running.

So how long do you really have to fix a finding? Work backwards from next year's audit. The auditor will test transactions from your next fiscal year. Every month the old process keeps running is a month of transactions that can produce the same finding again. The practical deadline is the start of your next fiscal year, and earlier is better.

Step five: next year's follow-up

At the next audit, you prepare the Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings. For each prior finding it says whether it is fully corrected, partially corrected or not corrected, and what you did. The auditor follows up on it, tests the area again, and if your schedule says "fully corrected" when it wasn't, that misstatement becomes a finding of its own.

This is the precise thing that happens a year later. The auditor reads what your corrective action plan said would change, pulls a sample from after the change, and checks for the evidence. If it's there, the finding closes. If it isn't, the same finding comes back as a repeat, and repeat findings are what funders weigh most heavily.

What you can do in the quiet months

Put the change in place in the first weeks after the report, not the last weeks before the next audit. Run a small self-test of your own after a couple of months, pulling transactions from after the change and checking each one has the evidence the plan promised. Keep the evidence where you can find it in a hurry, because "the document exists but we couldn't locate it during fieldwork" produces findings too.

And when the management decision arrives, file it with the finding. Next year's summary schedule has to reflect it, and if the funder asked for something your plan didn't include, the auditor will expect to see that as well.
