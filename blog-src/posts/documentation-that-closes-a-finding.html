A week before fieldwork, someone in finance writes a memo. It explains that the procedure has been updated, attaches the new policy and gets filed in the audit folder labelled "corrective action." It's well written and it closes nothing, because the auditor isn't going to test the memo.

Understanding what the auditor does test, when they follow up on a prior finding, is most of the work of closing one.

How the follow-up works

At each Single Audit, the auditor follows up on prior findings, starting from your Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings. For each finding you've marked corrected, they want to know whether that's true, and they find out the same way they found the problem: by testing transactions or records from the period after your fix took effect.

So the question the evidence has to answer is not "did the organization decide to fix this?" It's "did the fix operate, on real transactions, over enough time to be tested?"

What counts as evidence

Think of three layers, each doing a different job.

The design

The updated policy or procedure, approved by whoever has authority to approve it, with the date it took effect. This shows what the control is supposed to be. It's necessary and not sufficient: it tells the auditor what to look for in the next layer.

The operation

Evidence on the transactions themselves that the control ran. For a procurement finding, that's the quote comparison and the exclusion check attached to each purchase made after the change. For a payroll finding, the signed records of how time was split, with adjustments where they differed from the charges. For subrecipient monitoring, the dated invoice reviews and the risk assessments done before each new subaward.

This is the layer that closes findings. Each piece should carry a name and a date, and the date should come before the thing it controls. An approval signed after payment shows the control didn't work.

The monitoring

Evidence that you checked the control was working. A self-test where you pulled a sample of transactions after the change and confirmed each had the required evidence. A monthly review log. A note to the audit committee. This layer isn't strictly required in every case, but it is what makes your claim on the summary schedule credible and catches failures before the auditor does.

How long the fix has to run

Long enough to be sampled. If the auditor's sample is drawn from the whole fiscal year and your fix went live in the eleventh month, most of the sample comes from before it, and the finding is likely to repeat even if the fix is perfect.

That's why timing matters more than most organizations expect. A corrective action put in place in the first month after the report is received will have most of a year behind it at the next audit. The same action put in place the month before fieldwork will have almost nothing.

If your fix did go live late, don't hide it. Report the finding as partially corrected on the summary schedule, give the date the change took effect and show the evidence from after that date. Auditors and funders can work with an honest partial correction. They can't work with a claim of full correction that next year's sample contradicts.

Keeping it findable

One more practical point. Evidence that exists but can't be produced during fieldwork may as well not exist. Keep the support for each control with the transaction it relates to, in the same file or system, so that when the auditor selects a transaction, the evidence comes with it.

The organizations that close findings cleanly tend to have one thing in common: when the auditor asks for item 14 of 40, someone pulls it up in under a minute, and the approval is on it, with a name and a date from before the money moved.
