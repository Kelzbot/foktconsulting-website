Search for anything about audit findings and you will run into "the 5 C's": criteria, condition, cause, consequence and corrective action. Some versions say effect instead of consequence. The idea comes from the government auditing standards, the Yellow Book, which tells auditors what a well-developed finding contains, and it is the skeleton every Single Audit finding is built on.

Knowing the five parts is easy. The useful part is knowing which one your auditor spent the most time on, and which one you should spend the most time on, because they are not the same.

Criteria: the standard you were held to

The criteria are the rule. In a Single Audit that usually means a section of the Uniform Guidance, a term in your award, the program's own regulations, or your organization's written policy. If your policy says every purchase over $5,000 needs two signatures, the auditor can hold you to that even where the federal rule is looser, because a control you wrote down and don't follow is still a control that failed.

Check the citation. Now and then the criteria point to the wrong section, or to a requirement that doesn't apply to your award, and that is a legitimate thing to raise with the auditor before the report is final. It's rare, but it's the only part of the finding that can be wrong in a way that makes the rest of it disappear.

Condition: what they actually saw

The condition is the fact. Eight of sixty sampled payroll charges had no time and effort documentation. The quarterly financial report was submitted 41 days late. Two subrecipients were never risk-assessed before their subawards were signed.

A good condition is specific enough to be checked, and you should check it. Pull the same sample, look at the same documents and see whether you agree. If a document existed and simply wasn't provided during fieldwork, that is worth knowing, even if it is too late to change the report, because it tells you the problem may be retrieval rather than control.

Cause: the part that decides whether it comes back

This is the element most organizations read fastest, and it is the one that matters most to you. The criteria and condition describe the gap. The cause is supposed to explain why the gap exists, and your corrective action plan only works if it fixes the cause.

The trouble is that the cause written in a lot of findings is not really a cause. "Oversight." "Staff turnover." "The organization was not aware of the requirement." Each of these describes a moment, and none of them explains why the process allowed that moment to turn into a finding. People overlook things every day in organizations that never get findings, because something in the process catches the miss.

When I work on a finding I keep asking why until the answer is a missing step rather than a missing person. Approvals were skipped because of turnover, fine, but why did the new person not know to approve? Because the approval lives in one person's head and not in the workflow. Why is there no record when it does happen? Because the approval is an email reply, filed nowhere. Now you have a cause you can fix: build the approval into the step where the transaction is entered, so the transaction cannot move without it, and so the system keeps the record.

If your cause can be fixed by "reminding staff", it isn't the cause yet.

Effect: why anyone should care

The effect, or consequence, is the risk the gap creates. Sometimes it is concrete, unallowable costs charged to the award, or a report the agency relied on that was wrong. More often it is potential: without the control, unallowable costs could be charged and nobody would notice.

Effects matter because they shape how the finding is classified and how the funder reads it. A finding where the effect is "costs may be charged that are not supported" with zero questioned costs reads very differently from one where $180,000 couldn't be supported. Both need fixing. Only one of them comes with a repayment conversation.

Corrective action: the C you write

The first four C's are the auditor's. The fifth is yours. In a Single Audit the auditor's recommendation sits inside the finding, but the corrective action plan is a separate document the organization prepares, and the auditor isn't allowed to design it for you because they have to stay independent from the thing they will test next year.

A corrective action plan that works answers the cause line by line. It names the person who owns the fix, the specific change to the process, the date it will be in place and how you will know it is working. "We will strengthen our controls over procurement" does none of those things, which is exactly why plans written that way so often turn into repeat findings.

Reading your own finding in five minutes

Here is the order I'd read it in. Start with the condition and the sample size, to see how big the problem is. Check the criteria, to make sure the standard is the right one. Then spend the rest of your time on the cause, rewriting it in your own words until it points at a step in your process, because that rewritten sentence is the first line of your corrective action plan.

The effect will mostly take care of itself once the cause is fixed. The auditor will come back next year and test whether it was, and the test they run will be on whatever step you said you changed.
