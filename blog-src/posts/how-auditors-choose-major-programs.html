A Single Audit doesn't test all of your federal programs in depth. It tests a few of them, the major programs, and it tests those thoroughly. Which programs end up on that list isn't random, and once you understand how they're picked you can usually predict next year's major programs before your auditor tells you.

The risk-based approach

The method is in 2 CFR 200.518, and it works in steps. The auditor lists every federal program on your schedule of expenditures of federal awards, sorts them by size, assesses risk, and picks the programs where an audit is most likely to matter.

Step one: big and small

Programs are split into Type A and Type B by a dollar threshold that depends on your total federal expenditures. For most nonprofits spending a few million dollars a year, the Type A threshold is $1,000,000, so any program spending at least that much is Type A. Larger organizations have a higher threshold that scales with their total spending. Everything below the line is Type B.

Step two: are the big programs low risk?

The auditor then looks at each Type A program and decides whether it's low risk. Broadly, a Type A program can be considered low risk if it was audited as a major program in at least one of the two most recent audit periods and didn't have certain serious findings in the most recent one, like a material weakness, a modified opinion or significant questioned costs. The auditor also weighs judgement factors, such as how the program is run and whether it changed.

A Type A program that wasn't recently tested, or that had serious findings, is not low risk, and it will be a major program.

Step three: the small programs

Type B programs above a smaller threshold get their own risk assessment. The auditor isn't required to test every risky Type B, but they must select a number of high-risk Type B programs, tied to how many low-risk Type A programs there are.

Step four: coverage

Finally, the major programs together have to cover at least 40 percent of total federal expenditures, or 20 percent for a low-risk auditee. If the programs picked by risk don't add up to that, the auditor adds more until they do.

What that means in practice

Your largest program is usually tested. Even if it's low risk, coverage often pulls it in, because it's a big share of the total. If it had findings last year, it's tested by definition.

New programs tend to be tested. A Type A program that hasn't been audited as major recently can't qualify as low risk, and a new award is exactly that. So a big new grant in its first full year of spending is very likely to be a major program, at the moment when its processes are least settled.

Programs with prior findings come back. A material weakness or a modified opinion on a program keeps it off the low-risk list, which keeps it under the microscope until it produces a clean year.

And low-risk auditee status helps here too, because lowering the coverage floor from 40 to 20 percent often means fewer programs need to be added just to hit the number.

Using this to prepare

You can run a rough version of this yourself. List your programs by expected expenditure for the year, mark which crossed $1,000,000, note which had findings last year and which were major programs in the last two audits. The programs that are big, new or recently troubled are your likely major programs.

Then read the compliance requirements that apply to those programs, using the OMB Compliance Supplement's matrix, which I'll cover in another post. That tells you which areas the auditor will test on each one. Preparing those specific areas for those specific programs is a far better use of the months before fieldwork than trying to fix everything at once.

Ask your auditor at planning which programs they expect to treat as major. Most will tell you. It's not a secret, and knowing early gives you time to pull documentation together for the transactions they're about to sample.
