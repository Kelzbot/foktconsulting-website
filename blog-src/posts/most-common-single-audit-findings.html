Read enough Single Audit reports and the same handful of compliance areas keep turning up. The programs change, the organizations change, the auditors change, and the findings land in the same places. That's useful, because it means you can check yourself against a short list before your auditor does.

What follows are the areas I see most often in nonprofit findings, and the root cause I'd look for first in each. The cause is the part that matters. Every one of these has a version that a policy update won't fix.

Allowable costs and documentation

This is the big one. The finding usually says some sampled charges couldn't be supported: no invoice, no approval, no basis for how a shared cost was split between awards. Payroll is the most frequent source, specifically salaries charged to federal awards without records showing how the person's time was actually spent.

The root cause is almost always that documentation is gathered after the charge is posted, if at all. The charge goes on the award first and the support is supposed to follow, and sometimes it doesn't. The fix is to reverse the order, so a charge can't reach the award until the support is attached to it.

Subrecipient monitoring

If you pass federal money on to other organizations, you take on duties under 2 CFR 200.332: assess each subrecipient's risk before the subaward, put the required information in the subaward agreement, monitor their performance and spending, and follow up on their audit findings. Findings arrive when any of those steps can't be shown to have happened.

The root cause tends to be that subrecipient monitoring is treated as a program relationship rather than a financial control. Program staff talk to subrecipients constantly, but the risk assessment was never written down, invoices were paid without a documented review and nobody checked whether the subrecipient's own Single Audit had findings.

Procurement, suspension and debarment

The procurement standards in 2 CFR 200.317 to 200.327 expect written procedures, documented competition at the right thresholds, a record of how vendors were selected and a check that a vendor isn't excluded from federal work before you contract with them. Findings usually say the file for a purchase was incomplete: no quotes, no justification for a sole source, no exclusion check.

The root cause is usually that the purchase happened in one system and the paperwork was supposed to happen somewhere else. The requisition didn't ask for the comparison, so it wasn't done, or it was done and never filed.

Reporting

Federal financial reports and performance reports have deadlines, and they have to agree with your books. Findings say reports were late, or that the numbers in a submitted report couldn't be traced back to the general ledger.

The root cause is generally that the report is prepared from a spreadsheet that drifted away from the accounting system, with no reconciliation and no second person reviewing it before it was submitted. When the auditor asks how the number was produced, nobody can rebuild it.

Equipment and property

Equipment bought with federal money has to be tracked: description, identification number, source of funding, location, condition, and a physical inventory at least once every two years. Findings say the inventory was never done, or the property records don't match what's on the floor.

The root cause is that nobody owns the list. Equipment gets bought by programs, received by operations and paid by finance, and the record that ties them together belongs to no one in particular.

Cash management, period of performance and the rest

Less common but worth checking: drawing federal cash well ahead of when you need it, charging costs outside the award's period of performance, and eligibility findings on programs where participants have to qualify. Each of these has the same shape as the others. A rule that should be checked at the moment of the transaction is checked later, or not at all.

What these have in common

Look back at the root causes and they're nearly the same sentence written five ways. The control exists in someone's head or in a policy document, not at the step where the transaction happens, and nothing records that the control ran.

That's good news if you're preparing for an audit. You don't need five different projects. For each area that applies to your major programs, find the step where the money moves, put the check in that step, and make sure the check leaves a record your auditor can sample. That record is the thing next year's finding will or won't be written about.
