Every organization with a finding makes a decision about it, even if nobody calls it a decision. Either the finding gets fixed properly, with a change to the process and evidence that the change works, or it gets the minimum: a corrective action plan, an updated policy, and hope. The second option feels cheaper. It usually isn't, and the costs just arrive later and in different places.

What one finding rarely costs

Start with the reassuring part. A single finding, especially one without large questioned costs, almost never costs an organization its federal funding. Agencies and pass-through entities deal with findings all the time, and a first-time finding with a credible corrective action plan is a routine event in grants management.

The costs come from findings that stay.

What an unfixed finding costs

It comes back as a repeat

If the cause isn't fixed, next year's sample finds the same gap, and the finding returns with "repeat" attached. A repeat says something a first finding doesn't: that the organization was told and the fix didn't work. It's read as a management issue, not a transaction issue.

It can cost you low-risk status

If the finding is a material weakness, involves a modified opinion on a major program, or carries questioned costs above five percent of a program's expenditures, it keeps you out of low-risk auditee status for two years. Without that status the auditor must cover more of your federal spending, which usually means more major programs, more fieldwork and a bigger audit.

Audits get more expensive

Auditors bill time. Messy records take longer to test. Repeat findings lead to expanded testing in the areas where they occur. Fewer low-risk programs mean more programs tested. None of these show up as a line called "cost of the finding," but over a few years they add up in your audit fees, and in the staff time spent pulling documents for a larger audit.

Awards come with conditions

Under 2 CFR 200.208, a federal agency or pass-through entity can attach specific conditions to an award when an organization's history of compliance warrants it. Those can include requiring reimbursement instead of advance payments, more detailed or more frequent financial reports, additional monitoring, requiring prior approval for things you previously decided yourself, or technical assistance you didn't ask for. Each one adds work for your team and slows down how you spend the money.

Competitiveness slips

Before making an award, a federal agency reviews the risk an applicant poses, and audit findings from the Federal Audit Clearinghouse are part of that review. A strong proposal from an organization with three years of repeat findings is competing with a handicap that never appears in the scoring rubric. Pass-through entities do the same when deciding whom to fund and how closely to watch them.

The money itself

Finally, questioned costs that can't be supported may be disallowed in the management decision, and disallowed costs have to be repaid or offset from other funds. That's the most direct cost, and with good documentation it's often the most avoidable.

What fixing it costs

Fixing a finding properly means finding the real cause, changing the step in the process where the transaction happens, making the change leave evidence and then testing it yourself before the auditor does. That takes focused work, usually a few weeks of attention from someone who knows the rules and someone who knows your process.

It's a one-time cost. Once the step changes and the evidence is there, the finding closes at the next follow-up, and none of the costs above keep compounding.
