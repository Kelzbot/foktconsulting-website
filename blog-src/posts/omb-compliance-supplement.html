Most nonprofit finance teams have never opened the OMB Compliance Supplement. Most auditors couldn't do a Single Audit without it. That gap is one of the easiest advantages to take back, because the Supplement tells you, program by program, what your auditor is going to check.

What it is

The Compliance Supplement is published by the Office of Management and Budget and referenced in Appendix XI to 2 CFR 200. It's the document auditors use to identify the compliance requirements that matter for each federal program and to design their tests. OMB updates it each year, and the version that applies to an audit is generally the one in effect for that audit period.

It's long, hundreds of pages, but you only ever need a small part of it.

How it's organized

The parts that matter most to an auditee are these.

Part 2 is a matrix. Down the side it lists federal programs by Assistance Listing number. Across the top it lists the types of compliance requirements, each with a letter. A "Y" in a cell means that requirement is subject to audit for that program. This page tells you, at a glance, which areas the auditor will test on each of your programs if it's selected as a major program.

Part 3 describes each type of compliance requirement in general terms: what the rule is, where it comes from in 2 CFR 200, and the audit objectives and suggested procedures.

Part 4 has agency program-specific sections. If your program has its own section, it explains the program's particular rules, like who's eligible, what costs are allowed and which reports are due, along with any special tests the auditor should run.

Part 5 covers clusters of programs that are audited together, like research and development, and Part 6 describes what the auditor looks for in internal control. Part 7 tells auditors how to approach programs that aren't included in the Supplement at all.

How to use it on your own programs

Find your major programs, or the ones you expect to be major, in the Part 2 matrix by their Assistance Listing number. Note the requirements marked "Y". That's the list your auditor will work from.

Then read the Part 4 section for each program, if there is one. This is where the specific, testable details live, and the special tests in particular can catch organizations off guard, because they're unique to the program and not covered by general grants training.

Finally, for each requirement on your list, read the audit objectives and suggested procedures in Part 3 or Part 4. They tell you, in fairly plain terms, what evidence the auditor will ask for. If the procedure says the auditor will select a sample of subawards and verify that a risk assessment was documented before each was issued, you know exactly what you need to be able to produce.

If a program isn't in the Supplement, Part 7 applies, and the auditor works from the award terms and the general requirements instead. It's worth knowing that early, because it usually means more conversation with your auditor about what will be tested.

Why this year's ran late

The Supplement normally arrives in the spring or summer. The 2026 edition didn't. As of mid-September 2026 only a planning draft had been released, and auditors were advised not to issue reports for June 30, 2026 year-end audits on the basis of the draft. That left a lot of audits waiting on a document neither side controlled.

Check the current status before your fieldwork. If the final version is out, find out what changed from the draft for your programs. If it's still pending when your audit is scheduled, talk to your auditor early about timing, because a delay in the Supplement can push back your report, and your 30-day submission clock only starts when you receive it.

The advantage it gives you

An organization that has read its programs' sections walks into the planning meeting already knowing what will be tested. It can pull a sample of its own transactions for each of those requirements and check them against the suggested procedures months before the auditor does. Anything it finds is a problem it can fix quietly, instead of one that gets written up and published.
