A first Single Audit with five or six findings is common, and it rarely means the organization was careless. It usually means the organization grew faster than its back office. The finance processes that worked perfectly well at $300,000 of federal money are suddenly being held to rules written for organizations that manage millions, and the audit is the first time anyone checked.

Knowing where those gaps tend to be is most useful in the year before the first audit, while there's still time to close them.

The written procedures don't exist

The Uniform Guidance expects certain procedures to be documented, not just practised. Procurement is the clearest example: under 2 CFR 200.318, you need documented procurement procedures that reflect applicable law and conform to the federal procurement standards, and written standards of conduct covering conflicts of interest for the people involved in selecting and awarding contracts.

A small organization usually has a way of buying things. It often doesn't have it written down, and it almost never has the federal pieces in it, like the thresholds where competition is required or the check that a vendor isn't excluded from federal work. The audit finds the gap the first time it samples a purchase.

Payroll charged by budget

When a new federal award arrives, the budget assigns staff to it by percentage: 40 percent of the program manager, 20 percent of the finance officer. Payroll gets set up to charge those percentages and nobody revisits them.

The rule on compensation, 2 CFR 200.430(i), requires charges to be based on records that accurately reflect the work actually performed. If the program manager actually spent 60 percent of their time on the award in some months and 20 in others, budget percentages don't meet that standard, and there's no record showing they do. This is the single most common first-audit finding I'd expect to see.

Subrecipient monitoring as a relationship

Organizations that pass money on often treat their subrecipients as partners, which they are, and manage them through program staff who talk to them all the time. What's missing is the paper side: a documented risk assessment before the subaward, a review of invoices against the budget before payment, and a check of the subrecipient's own audit. The relationship is good. The evidence of monitoring isn't there.

The schedule of expenditures

The schedule of expenditures of federal awards is prepared by the organization, not the auditor, under 2 CFR 200.510(b). It lists every federal award by program, with Assistance Listing numbers, pass-through entity identifiers and amounts expended, and it's the foundation the auditor uses to choose major programs.

First-time auditees often build it for the first time at year-end, from a general ledger that wasn't set up to track awards cleanly. Federal money that came through a state agency gets left off, or two awards under the same program get merged. Errors here can push the audit off course before fieldwork starts.

Spreadsheets with no review trail

A lot of grants management at small organizations lives in spreadsheets: budget-to-actual trackers, allocation calculations, report workings. The spreadsheet itself isn't the problem. The problem is that nobody reviews it, versions overwrite each other, and when the auditor asks how a number in a federal report was produced, the version that produced it no longer exists.

Closing the gaps early

If you know your first Single Audit is coming, the year before is the time to act, because the auditor will test transactions from the whole audit year. A fix made in month eleven doesn't help the ten months before it.

Start with the two areas that produce the most findings. Write down your procurement procedures with the federal thresholds and the exclusion check in them, and start using them for every federally funded purchase. Then set up a simple, regular record of how staff time is actually split across awards, signed by someone who knows, with adjustments when it doesn't match what was charged.

Build the schedule of expenditures quarterly rather than once at year-end, so problems surface while they're small. And for any spreadsheet that feeds a federal report, keep the version used for each submission with a note of who reviewed it.

None of this needs new software. It needs a few processes to be written down, done the same way every time and leave a trace, which is what the auditor is looking for in the first place.
