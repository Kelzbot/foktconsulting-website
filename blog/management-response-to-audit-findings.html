How to write a management response to an audit finding, and when to disagree

November 5, 2026 · 3 min read · Kelvin Fawehinmi

Near the bottom of every Single Audit finding there is a short paragraph titled "views of responsible officials" or "management's response." It is the only part of the finding written in your words, and it gets less attention than almost anything else in the process. Usually it is drafted in an afternoon by whoever was in the exit meeting.

It deserves more than that, because it is published with the finding and read by people deciding whether to fund you.

What the response is for

The auditor includes your response so a reader can see the organization's position next to the auditor's. It is not the corrective action plan, although the two should agree. The plan is the detailed document. The response is a short statement that tells the reader whether you agree and, in a sentence or two, what you're doing about it.

Think about who reads it. Federal agencies look at audit results when assessing the risk of a new award, and pass-through entities look when deciding how closely to monitor a subrecipient. The person reading your response probably doesn't know you, doesn't know your staff and has a stack of other applications. They are reading for one signal: does this organization take the problem seriously and know how to fix it.

What a good response says

Keep it short and specific. A strong response does a few things. It states plainly whether management agrees. It names the cause in your own words, ideally more precisely than the auditor did. It describes the change, who owns it and when it took or will take effect.

"Management agrees. The approval for indirect cost allocations was given by email and not recorded with the journal entry, so the evidence could not be produced for 6 of the 40 entries sampled. Since March 1, allocation entries cannot be posted without the approver's sign-off in the accounting system. Owner: Finance Manager."

That's the whole thing. It shows the organization understands its own process, it gives a date the auditor can test from, and it reads as calm.

What it shouldn't say

Don't blame people. "The former grants accountant failed to follow procedures" is the response I'd most want to delete, because it tells the reader that the control depended on one person and nothing has changed about the process. It also puts a colleague's failure on a public record forever.

Don't over-promise. "This will not recur" is a sentence the auditor will remember next year. Describe what changed and let the follow-up speak for itself.

Don't argue in vague terms. "Management believes the finding overstates the issue" with nothing behind it reads as defensive, and a defensive response can make a reader trust the finding more, not less.

And don't promise a fix that's bigger than you will actually deliver. A response that commits to a new grants management system by June, when the budget for it doesn't exist, becomes next year's problem.

Can you disagree with an audit finding?

Yes. The Uniform Guidance expects it to happen. Under 2 CFR 200.511(c), if you don't agree with a finding or believe corrective action isn't required, your corrective action plan has to include an explanation and specific reasons.

The best time to disagree is before the report is final. Most disagreements are about facts: a document existed but wasn't found during fieldwork, a requirement doesn't apply to the award, a compensating control was operating. If you can show the evidence while the auditor is still drafting, the finding may change or disappear. After the report is issued, the finding stands as written, and your disagreement goes to the funder through the response and the plan.

When you do disagree in writing, do it the way you'd want to read it. Quote the criteria, state the fact you think was missed, and attach or reference the evidence.

"Management does not agree. The award terms for this program waive the prior approval requirement for rebudgeting below 10 percent of the total budget (Award Terms, section 4.2). All three sampled rebudgets fell below that limit. Documentation has been provided to the awarding agency."

The funder decides in the management decision. A clear, evidence-backed disagreement gives them something they can sustain or clear. A vague one leaves them no choice but to sustain the finding.

Agreeing in part

Partial agreement is common and fine. You can agree that the control was missing while disagreeing with the questioned costs because you have since produced documentation for some of them. Say exactly which part you accept and which part you don't, so the funder can resolve each one.

The response is the one paragraph in your audit that you control completely. Two careful sentences there will be read many more times than the hour it takes to write them.
