How to write a corrective action plan that actually closes a Single Audit finding

October 29, 2026 · 4 min read · Kelvin Fawehinmi

The most common corrective action plan I see says some version of "management will strengthen internal controls over this area and provide additional training to staff." It is polite, it is short, and it is the reason a lot of findings come back the following year with the word repeat attached.

A corrective action plan is not a promise to try harder. It is a short technical document that tells your funder, and next year's auditor, exactly what changed and how they can check it.

Who writes it

You do. The organization prepares the corrective action plan, not the auditor. That surprises a lot of people, especially first-time auditees who assume the firm that found the problem will tell them how to fix it.

The reason is independence. Your auditor will test the fix next year, and an auditor cannot design a process and then audit it. They will give you a recommendation inside the finding, and many will happily talk through whether your plan addresses the problem, but the plan itself is yours.

What the rule requires

Under 2 CFR 200.511(c), the corrective action plan has to cover each finding in the current year's report and include the name of the contact person responsible for the corrective action, the action planned, and the anticipated completion date. If you disagree with a finding, or think no action is needed, the plan must say so and give specific reasons.

That's the floor. It is possible to meet every word of it and still write a plan that changes nothing, which is why the floor is not the useful standard.

What a plan that works contains

An owner with a title, not a department

"Finance department" cannot be held accountable. "Controller, Jane Smith" can. Name the role and the person, so that when someone leaves, the plan makes it obvious whose job transfers.

A change to a step, not a policy

Most findings are caused by a step that doesn't exist or doesn't leave a record. Updating the policy manual is fine, but the auditor doesn't sample your policy manual next year. They sample transactions. So the plan has to describe the change at the point where the transaction happens: who approves, in what system, before what, and where the approval is recorded.

Compare these two lines for a procurement finding.

"Management will ensure compliance with federal procurement standards."

"From January 1, any purchase over the micro-purchase threshold cannot be entered in the accounting system without an attached quote comparison and a SAM.gov exclusion check printout. The purchasing coordinator attaches both, and the finance manager approves in the system before a purchase order number is issued."

The second one tells next year's auditor exactly what to look for, and tells your staff exactly what to do on Monday.

A date for the change and a date for the proof

Two dates matter, and most plans give one. The first is when the new step goes live. The second is when you will have evidence that it has been running long enough to test. A fix that goes live in the last month of your fiscal year will barely appear in next year's sample, and the auditor may find the old problem in the eleven months before it.

How you will know it works

Say how you'll check it yourself before the auditor does. A simple self-test works: after the change has run for a couple of months, pull 25 transactions from after the change date and confirm each one carries the evidence the plan promised. Keep that test on file. If it fails, you have found out in time to fix it, rather than finding out from next year's report.

Write it for the reader you'll never meet

Your corrective action plan is part of the reporting package that goes to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse, where it is public. A program officer at an agency deciding on your next award, or a pass-through entity deciding how much to monitor you, may read it years from now without ever talking to you.

That reader is asking one question: does this organization understand what went wrong and has it actually fixed it? A specific plan with a named owner and a dated change answers yes. A vague plan with a promise to strengthen controls answers maybe.

If you disagree

You're allowed to. The rule expects you to explain the disagreement with specific reasons, and a calm factual explanation with evidence is much more effective than a defensive one. I'll cover how to write that response in its own post, because the tone of it matters as much as the facts.

If the plan is accepted, the funder's management decision will say so. If it isn't, you'll hear what they expect instead. Either way, the version of you that writes next year's Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings will be grateful that this year's plan said exactly what changed, because that is the document where you report whether it worked.
