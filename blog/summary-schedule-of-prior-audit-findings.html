The Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings: your own status report, and why overclaiming backfires

November 12, 2026 · 3 min read · Kelvin Fawehinmi

Of all the documents in a Single Audit reporting package, the Summary Schedule of Prior Audit Findings is the one most likely to be written in a hurry, the night before it's due, by copying last year's corrective action plan and changing the tense. It is also a document your auditor tests, and getting it wrong can create a brand new finding.

What it is

The summary schedule is the organization's own report on the status of every finding from the prior audit. You prepare it, not the auditor. It goes in the reporting package to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse alongside the current year's findings and your corrective action plan for them.

Under 2 CFR 200.511(b), it has to report the status of all audit findings included in the prior audit's schedule of findings and questioned costs. It also covers findings from earlier years that you previously reported as not fully corrected, so an old problem stays on the schedule until it is actually closed.

What each status requires

When a finding is fully corrected, the schedule lists it and says so. That's the simplest entry and, as we'll see, the one that needs the most evidence behind it.

When a finding is not corrected or only partially corrected, the schedule describes why it recurred and what corrective action is planned, along with any partial corrective action already taken. If the corrective action is significantly different from what you said in the prior year's plan, or from what the funder required in its management decision, you explain why.

There is also a narrow case where a finding can be reported as no longer valid or not warranting further action. The rule sets conditions for that, including that two years have passed since the reporting package with the finding was submitted, that the federal agency or pass-through entity isn't currently following up, and that no management decision was issued. If all of those apply, you can say so with the reasons. It isn't a general way to make an inconvenient finding go away.

How the auditor tests it

The auditor follows up on prior findings as part of the current audit and uses your schedule as the starting point. They read your status for each one and then test whether it's true, usually by sampling transactions in the area from after the date you say the fix took effect.

Here's the part that catches people out. If the auditor concludes that the summary schedule materially misrepresents the status of a prior finding, they report that as a current-year finding. So an organization that writes "fully corrected" for a finding that isn't can end up with two findings: the original issue reported again as a repeat, and a new one saying its own status report was wrong.

The summary schedule is the one place in the audit where you grade yourself, and the auditor checks the grading.

Why overclaiming happens

Usually not dishonesty. More often it's optimism plus timing. The corrective action plan said "new review step by March 31," the step was introduced in March, so the finding is marked fully corrected. Nobody checked whether the step actually ran on every transaction since March, or whether the evidence of it was being kept.

Another common cause is the schedule being written from the plan rather than from the evidence. The plan describes what should have happened. The schedule should describe what did.

Writing it well

Before marking anything fully corrected, do the auditor's test yourself. Pull a sample of transactions from after the change date, check that each one has the approval, document or record the fix was supposed to produce, and keep the results. If every item passes, fully corrected is an honest status with evidence behind it.

If some items fail, or the change went live too late in the year to have much of a track record, say partially corrected and be specific.

"Partially corrected. Since October 1, subrecipient invoices cannot be paid until the program manager's review is recorded in the payment request. Transactions before October 1 followed the prior process. A self-test of 20 invoices paid between October 1 and December 31 found the review recorded on all 20."

A funder reading that understands exactly where you are. An auditor reading it knows what to test. And if next year's follow-up confirms it, the finding closes cleanly with nothing on the record that you'll have to explain later.

Keep the schedule consistent with the management decision too. If the funder asked for something your plan didn't include, the schedule should show you did it, or explain why not.
